Unit 1 · C01

The GPU economy: cost per used hour

From the lessonC01 §2

One GPU costs 25,000 dollars and lasts 4 years. It draws 1.4 kW. Power costs 0.10 dollars per kWh. Staff costs 0.10 dollars per hour. Cost per GPU-hour spreads the fixed capex over used hours, then adds power and staff.

From the lessonC01 §6

At 40 percent utilization the capex part is 25,000 divided by 14,016 hours, or 1.78 dollars. Total cost is 2.02 dollars per GPU-hour. At 85 percent utilization the capex part is 0.84 dollars. Total cost is 1.08 dollars. Utilization more than doubled, and the unit cost nearly halved.

The lesson

Utilization sets the sticker price. The same GPU costs 2.02 dollars per hour at 40 percent utilization and 1.08 at 85 percent. Nothing about the chip changed. Only the share of hours sold changed.

Why it matters. Fixed cost spreads over used hours. Idle hours still cost money but earn none.

Transfer. Price the used hour, not the owned hour. Any asset with fixed cost and variable demand lives or dies on utilization.

Unit 2 · C02

Custom accelerators: the NRE break-even

From the lessonC02 §2

A custom chip needs 50M dollars of NRE before the first wafer. It saves 0.82 dollars per chip-hour against a GPU. At 85 percent utilization one chip runs 7,446 hours per year and saves 6,105.72 dollars per year.

From the lessonC02 §6

Break-even fleet size is 50,000,000 divided by 6,105.72, or 8,189 chips. Below about 8,200 chips, buy GPUs. Above, the custom chip wins. At 20,000 chips the annual saving is 122M dollars against the 50M NRE.

The lesson

Custom silicon is a break-even problem. The 50M NRE is a fixed cost. Each chip is a unit of contribution. 8,189 chips is the break-even quantity, straight from the U01 formula.

Why it matters. Chip strategy looks like deep technology but decides like unit economics. The question is never whether the chip is better. It is whether the fleet is big enough.

Transfer. Turn every fixed-cost bet into a break-even quantity. Then ask whether you will ever reach it.

Unit 3 · C03

The roofline: memory or compute binds

From the lessonC03 §5

A chip with peak 300 TFLOP/s and memory bandwidth 2 TB/s hits a ridge at 150 FLOP per byte. Below the ridge the memory door binds. Above it the compute binds. Each byte moved can feed at most I FLOPs.

From the lessonC03 §6

Workload A has intensity 100. Attainable speed is the minimum of 300 and 200, so 200 TFLOP/s, memory-bound, 67 percent of peak. Workload B has intensity 500. Attainable is 300 TFLOP/s, compute-bound. The decode step at intensity 40 gives 80 TFLOP/s: the door, not the cooks.

The lesson

Find the binding minimum. Three numbers describe the system, but only the smallest decides the speed. The roofline makes the binding constraint visible: 200 TFLOP/s for workload A, not 300.

Why it matters. Money spent raising a non-binding stage buys nothing. The 300 TFLOP/s peak is irrelevant to a memory-bound workload.

Transfer. In any pipeline, find the minimum first and spend there. Benchmarks of the non-binding parts are decoration.

Unit 4 · C04

Lifecycle cost: plan, build, operate, refresh

From the lessonC04 §2

A 100 MW site has four phases. Plan: 5M over 6 months. Build: 400M over 18 months. Operate: 40M per year for 6 years. Refresh: 120M in year 7. Undiscounted total is 765M dollars.

From the lessonC04 §6

At 10 percent, with build centered at year 1.5 and opex in years 2 to 7: PV build 346.7M, PV opex 158.4M, PV refresh 61.6M, PV plan 5M. Total PV is 571.7M dollars. The refresh, large in sticker terms, is the smallest PV line. Late costs matter less.

Unit 5 · C05

Power caps the site: the queue appears

From the lessonC05 §5

Each GPU needs 1.4 kW of facility power. A 100 MW site holds at most 71,428 GPUs. The constraint is physical, not priced: N times p_gpu must stay under site power.

From the lessonC05 §6

Demand at 70 MW: served 70, wait 0. Demand jumps to 140 MW: served 100, wait 40 MW, which is 28,571 GPU-equivalents waiting. The site did not get slower. It got full. The demand curve now has a queue attached.

Unit 6 · C06

Cooling: the 2.28-year payback

From the lessonC06 §5

Air cooling: 100 kW of IT load at PUE 1.5 needs 150 kW from the meter. Liquid cooling at PUE 1.15 needs 115 kW. The saving is 35 kW, worth 30,660 dollars per year at 0.10 dollars per kWh.

From the lessonC06 §6

Liquid costs 70,000 dollars more per rack. Payback is 70,000 divided by 30,660, or 2.28 years. Over a 6-year rack life, liquid saves 113,960 dollars per rack. At 1,000 racks the saving is 114M dollars. That is why gigawatt builds choose liquid despite the capex.

The lesson

Capex that pays in years is cheap capex. A 70,000 dollar premium per rack returns 30,660 per year and pays back in 2.28 years. At 1,000 racks it returns 114M dollars over the rack life.

Why it matters. Cooling is a multiplier on every watt the site will ever buy. A one-time premium buys a permanent discount on the meter.

Transfer. Judge infrastructure capex by payback years, not by sticker. Anything under half the asset life deserves a hard look.

Unit 7 · C07

Gigawatt scale: concrete and electrons

From the lessonC07 §5

A 1 GW site at 10 dollars per watt costs 10B dollars to build. It holds 714,285 GPUs at 1.4 kW each, or the power of 800,000 homes at 1.25 kW each.

From the lessonC07 §6

Annual energy is 1,000,000 kW times 8,760 hours times 0.08 dollars, or 700.8M dollars per year. Over 10 years undiscounted, energy is 7B dollars, comparable to the build. The two big numbers are concrete and electrons, in that order.

Unit 8 · C08

Build or lease: the 48 percent crossover

From the lessonC08 §5

Build: 30M capex plus 1M per year of maintenance. The annuity factor at 10 percent over 4 years is 3.1699. Build PV is 33.17M dollars.

From the lessonC08 §6

Lease at 2.50 dollars per GPU-hour for 1,000 GPUs. At 20 percent utilization, lease PV is 13.88M. Lease wins by 19.29M. At 80 percent utilization, lease PV is 55.54M. Build wins by 22.37M. Crossover utilization is 33.17 divided by 69.42, or 0.478. Build wins above about 48 percent.

5Nugget 5 · From the lesson C08 §6
Nugget 5 · From the lesson C08 §6
The lesson

Ownership has a utilization threshold. At 20 percent utilization, leasing wins by 19.29M. At 80 percent, building wins by 22.37M. The crossover sits at 48 percent.

Why it matters. Fixed cost punishes idleness and rewards full use. The lease is pure variable cost, so it wins exactly when the asset would sit idle.

Transfer. Never choose build versus lease without a utilization forecast. The threshold, not the sticker, decides.

Unit 9 · C09

Utilization compounds: sticker versus effective

From the lessonC09 §5

The sticker price is not the used price. Effective cost per used hour is the sticker divided by utilization. The total cancels. The ratio is all that matters.

From the lessonC09 §6

Sticker 2.02 dollars at 40 percent utilization: effective 5.06 dollars per used hour. Sticker 1.08 at 85 percent: effective 1.27. The sticker already spreads fixed cost once, and the effective cost spreads it again. Low utilization hurts twice.

Unit 11 · C11

Supply concentration: the HHI arithmetic

From the lessonC11 §5

Market shares of 70, 15, 10, 5 give an HHI of 4,900 plus 225 plus 100 plus 25, or 5,250. Highly concentrated. Squaring makes the index rise faster than the largest share.

From the lessonC11 §6

Split the leader into two 35-percent firms: HHI becomes 1,225 plus 1,225 plus 225 plus 100 plus 25, or 2,800. Still high, but nearly halved. The arithmetic shows why two strong suppliers are a different world from one.

Unit 12 · C12

Scenarios: decide by regret, not by hope

From the lessonC12 §5

Build PV is 33.17M, fixed. Lease PV at 60 percent utilization over 4 years depends on the lease rate: 24.99M at 1.50, 41.65M at 2.50, 58.31M at 3.50 dollars per GPU-hour.

From the lessonC12 §6

Build wins 2 of 3 scenarios and never loses by more than 8.18M. Lease wins 1 scenario and loses by up to 25.14M. Minimizing maximum regret picks build: the option that never fails badly.

The lesson

Choose the option that never fails badly. Build loses at most 8.18M across the three scenarios. Lease loses up to 25.14M. The resilient pick is build, even though lease wins one scenario.

Why it matters. Expected value needs probabilities you do not have. Regret needs only the table. When the future is a set of stories, minimize the worst story.

Transfer. When probabilities are guesses, decide by regret. The safe choice is the one whose worst case you can survive.
